## Lesson Overview

This lesson reframes "networking" for teenagers away from the anxiety-inducing, transactional image many of them have absorbed (cold LinkedIn messages, "hustle culture" content, mass outreach). Instead, students learn that professional networking at this age is about **nurturing real relationships they already have** — teachers, coaches, family friends, alumni of their school, instructors from Lumexa or other programs — and communicating with them respectfully and clearly.

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. Explain the difference between networking and cold/mass messaging strangers.
2. Identify 3-5 real, existing contacts appropriate for professional outreach.
3. Write a polite, well-structured introduction or outreach message using a repeatable formula.
4. Apply professional communication etiquette (tone, timing, follow-up, gratitude) across email, Google Docs comments, and LinkedIn.
5. State the lesson's safety norms from memory and apply them to their own outreach plan.

## Safety Framing (Non-Negotiable — Read Before Class)

This lesson is about **existing, known, real-world connections** — not meeting or messaging strangers. Instructors must not let the lesson drift into "add strangers on LinkedIn to grow your network" territory. Reinforce constantly:

- Every outreach message a student plans to send should be **known to a trusted adult** (parent, guardian, or the instructor) before or as it is sent.
- Students **never** arrange to meet anyone from online outreach in person alone. If an outreach contact suggests meeting, a trusted adult is involved in planning it (and in practice, at this age, almost all "networking" contact stays in writing/video, not in-person solo meetings).
- LinkedIn has a **16+ minimum age**. Under 16, or any student/family who prefers not to use LinkedIn, uses the portfolio-first alternative (a personal portfolio site or PDF one-pager + email) instead. Do not pressure any student to create a LinkedIn account.
- Students should never share home address, school schedule/location details, financial information, or passwords in any outreach message.
- If any contact's reply ever feels off, pushy, overly personal, or makes a student uncomfortable, the instruction is simple: **stop responding and tell a trusted adult immediately.** This is not a failure on the student's part — it is the correct, expected action.

## Teaching Sequence

### 1. Warm-Open & Reframe (5 min)
Ask: "When you hear the word 'networking,' what comes to mind?" Expect answers like "awkward," "asking strangers for jobs," "LinkedIn spam," "used-car-salesman energy." Validate that this reaction is common and reasonable — because most of what teens see modeled online *is* that.

Reframe: "Real networking, especially at your age, is just **staying in touch with people who already know you, in a way that's clear and respectful.** It's not messaging 500 strangers. It's writing one thoughtful message to your old robotics coach."

### 2. Networking vs. Mass Cold-Messaging (8 min)
Present the contrast directly (write on shared screen or slide):

| Networking (what we do) | Mass cold-messaging (what we don't do) |
|---|---|
| Reaching out to someone who already knows you or is one warm introduction away | Messaging strangers you have no connection to, hoping one responds |
| One thoughtful, specific message | Copy-pasting the same generic message to dozens of people |
| A real reason tied to a shared context ("you taught my Intro to Python class") | No context, just "let's connect" |
| No immediate ask, or a small, specific, low-pressure ask | Asking a stranger for a job/favor immediately |
| Trusted adult is aware | No one else knows the outreach is happening |

Talking point: "Quality over quantity isn't just etiquette — it's what actually works. One genuine message to your former teacher who remembers you will get a warmer, more useful reply than fifty generic messages to strangers who don't know you and have no reason to respond."

### 3. Live Demo: A Polite Introduction Message (12 min)
Screen-share a Google Doc. Build the message live, narrating each choice, using the fictional example below (also in the Student Lesson).

**Scenario:** Jordan R., 17, wants to reach back out to Ms. Alvarez, who taught Jordan's sophomore-year intro coding elective, to ask a small, specific question about a summer program.

**Demo message (write it live, line by line, narrating why):**

> Subject: Question from a former student — Jordan R.
>
> Hi Ms. Alvarez,
>
> I hope your school year is off to a good start! This is Jordan Reyes — I was in your Intro to Coding elective sophomore year (I sat near the front, and did the maze-solving robot project).
>
> I'm applying to a summer coding program this year and remembered how much I enjoyed your class. Would you be open to answering one or two quick questions about how you got started in tech, whenever you have a few minutes? No pressure at all if you're busy — I completely understand.
>
> Thank you for considering it, and I hope you're doing well!
>
> Best,
> Jordan Reyes

Narrate the structure as you go: **who you are + shared context → genuine reason for reaching out → small, specific, low-pressure ask → gracious close → full name.** Point out what's absent: no attachment of personal documents yet, no request for a job, no urgency/pressure language, no personal contact info beyond what's needed.

Then show a **sample reply** and model a **thank-you follow-up** (see Student Lesson worked example) so students see the full loop, not just the opening message.

### 4. Etiquette Across Tools (8 min)
Briefly cover tool-specific norms:

- **Email:** Clear subject line, greeting, sign-off with full name, check spelling/tone before sending, reply within a few days if you receive a reply.
- **Google Docs (comments/shared drafts):** When commenting on a shared doc (e.g., a mentor reviewing your resume), keep comments specific and gracious ("Thanks for catching that — fixed!"), don't @-mention people repeatedly, resolve comments once addressed.
- **LinkedIn (16+ only):** Always send a connection request with a short personal note (never blank), use real name and a professional-looking photo or none at all, keep the profile portfolio-first (projects, skills) rather than treating it like a general social feed.
- **Portfolio-first alternative (under 16 / opt-out):** A one-page site or PDF resume + email is fully equivalent for this lesson's purposes — reassure students this is not a lesser option.

### 5. Guided Activity Launch (15 min)
Students open their own Google Doc (from the Worksheet) and:
1. List 3-5 real people from their existing network.
2. Pick one.
3. Draft one real introduction/outreach message using the formula.
4. Note how/when they'll tell a trusted adult about this outreach (before sending, ideally).

Circulate (or use breakout rooms if virtual) to review drafts one-on-one. Do **not** let students send the message during class — the message should go home for trusted-adult review first, unless a parent/guardian is present and reviewing live.

### 6. Discussion Questions (Weave in during activity or close, ~7 min total)
- "Why might a short, specific message get a better response than a long, vague one?"
- "What's the difference between asking someone to 'grab coffee and pick their brain' and asking one clear question?"
- "If someone you reached out to never replies, what should you do? What should you *not* do?" (Answer: it's fine, people are busy; don't send repeated follow-ups or take it personally; one polite follow-up after 1-2 weeks is the max.)
- "Why does a trusted adult need to know about this kind of outreach, even though it feels like 'just an email'?"

### 7. Lesson Close (5 min)
Recap the three big ideas: (1) networking = nurturing real, existing relationships, not mass-messaging strangers; (2) a good message is short, specific, and low-pressure; (3) a trusted adult is always in the loop on outreach. Preview that Lesson 7 will build on this by covering how to follow up and keep in touch over time.

## Common Misconceptions to Correct

- **"Networking means messaging as many people as possible."** Correct: one genuine message beats dozens of generic ones; quality and relevance matter far more than volume.
- **"I need to ask for something big right away (a job, an internship, a recommendation)."** Correct: the first message should have a small, low-pressure ask, or no ask at all beyond reconnecting — trust and relationships are built over multiple respectful interactions, not one transactional request.
- **"If I don't have a 'real' professional connection, I have no one to reach out to."** Correct: teachers, coaches, relatives' colleagues, past instructors, and school alumni all count as real network contacts at this age.
- **"Not hearing back means I did something wrong."** Correct: busy adults often don't reply immediately or at all; it is not personal and does not require an apology-toned follow-up.

## Anticipated Student Difficulties & Troubleshooting

| Difficulty | Instructor Response |
|---|---|
| Student can't think of anyone to list | Prompt with categories: teachers/coaches, family friends in any job, relatives, past camp/program instructors, school alumni they've met. Everyone has at least 2-3. |
| Message drafts are too long/rambling | Point back to the formula; ask "what's the one sentence version of your context, and the one sentence version of your ask?" |
| Student wants to ask for something big (e.g., a job) right away | Redirect gently: "Let's make the first message just about reconnecting or one small question — bigger asks come later, after there's a relationship again." |
| Student is anxious about "bothering" someone | Normalize: most adults are flattered when a former student reaches out respectfully; a polite, brief message is never a bother. |
| Student wants to add a stranger found online because they "seem successful" | Firmly redirect: this lesson is only about real, existing connections; cold-adding strangers is explicitly out of scope and discouraged. |
| Under-16 student feels excluded by LinkedIn examples | Reaffirm the portfolio-first path is equal in value; use their existing portfolio/PDF resume for the same exercise. |

## How to Review Student Work

Check each drafted message for:
- [ ] Names a real, specific person the student actually knows (not a stranger or invented person).
- [ ] States shared context clearly in the first 1-2 sentences.
- [ ] Has a genuine, low-pressure reason for the message.
- [ ] Ask (if any) is small and specific, not vague or large.
- [ ] Tone is warm, respectful, and appropriately brief (roughly 80-150 words).
- [ ] No personal safety information included (address, schedule, passwords, financial info).
- [ ] Student has identified a trusted adult and a plan to loop them in before sending.

Give feedback as a comment directly on the student's Google Doc so they practice receiving professional-style feedback in the same tool they'll use for real mentor review later in the course.
