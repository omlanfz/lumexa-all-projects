# Lesson 6: Networking and Professional Communication

**Course 18: Professional Presence & Opportunity Building — Digital Independence Path**

## What Is Networking, Really?

Forget the awkward image of "networking" you might have in your head — messaging strangers, forcing small talk, asking people you don't know for favors. That's not what this lesson is about, and it's not good networking anyway.

**Real networking, at your age, means staying in touch with people who already know you** — in a way that's clear, respectful, and genuine. It's your former teacher, a coach, a family friend who works in a field you're curious about, or an instructor from a program you took. You already have a network. This lesson is about using it well.

## Why It Matters

People are far more likely to help, advise, or remember someone they already have a real connection to than a stranger who messaged them out of nowhere. A short, thoughtful message to someone who knows you will almost always get a warmer response than a generic message blasted to fifty strangers. **Quality beats quantity, every time.**

## Networking Is NOT

- Messaging strangers you found online because they have a job you want.
- Copy-pasting the same message to dozens of people.
- Asking someone you just met for a big favor right away.
- Something you do without a trusted adult knowing.

## Safety Norms (Know These Before You Send Anything)

1. **A trusted adult (parent, guardian, or your instructor) should know about any outreach message before or as you send it.** This isn't about "asking permission" like you're a little kid — it's a normal professional practice: even adults loop in a mentor before an important email.
2. **You never meet someone from an online or written introduction in person alone.** If a conversation ever leads toward meeting up, that only happens with a trusted adult involved in the plan.
3. **LinkedIn requires you to be 16 or older.** If you're under 16, or you or your family prefer not to use it, you'll build the exact same skills using a personal portfolio page or a one-page PDF resume plus email — that path is just as legitimate.
4. **Never share your address, school schedule, passwords, or financial information** in an outreach message, no matter who it's to.
5. **If a reply ever feels strange, pushy, or makes you uncomfortable — stop responding and tell a trusted adult right away.** That is always the right move, never something to feel embarrassed about.

## The Introduction Message Formula

A good outreach message is short — usually 80-150 words — and follows this shape:

1. **Greeting + who you are + shared context** (how do they know you?)
2. **Genuine reason for reaching out** (why them, why now?)
3. **A small, specific, low-pressure ask** (or no ask at all — just reconnecting counts)
4. **Gracious close + your full name**

That's it. No hard sell, no long life story, no big ask on the first message.

## Worked Example

**Scenario:** Jordan R., 17, wants to reconnect with Ms. Alvarez, who taught Jordan's sophomore-year Intro to Coding elective, to ask a quick question about a summer program.

**Jordan's message:**

> Subject: Question from a former student — Jordan R.
>
> Hi Ms. Alvarez,
>
> I hope your school year is off to a good start! This is Jordan Reyes — I was in your Intro to Coding elective sophomore year (I sat near the front, and did the maze-solving robot project).
>
> I'm applying to a summer coding program this year and remembered how much I enjoyed your class. Would you be open to answering one or two quick questions about how you got started in tech, whenever you have a few minutes? No pressure at all if you're busy — I completely understand.
>
> Thank you for considering it, and I hope you're doing well!
>
> Best,
> Jordan Reyes

**Ms. Alvarez's reply (a few days later):**

> Hi Jordan,
>
> So good to hear from you — I remember that robot project well! Happy to help. Feel free to send your questions over whenever, or we could do a quick 15-minute call if that's easier. Good luck with the application!
>
> Ms. Alvarez

**Jordan's follow-up (same day):**

> Hi Ms. Alvarez,
>
> Thank you so much for getting back to me! A quick call would be great if you have time — I'll let my mom know so she can help me find a time that works, and I'll send a couple options over email.
>
> Thanks again,
> Jordan

Notice: Jordan mentions a trusted adult naturally as part of arranging a call — not as an afterthought. That's normal, professional, and safe, all at once.

## Activity: Draft Your Own Introduction Message

1. **List 3-5 real people already in your network** — a teacher, coach, family friend, relative, or past instructor. (Use the Worksheet to help brainstorm.)
2. **Pick one person** you'd genuinely like to reconnect with or ask a small question.
3. **Draft one real message to them** using the formula above, in a Google Doc.
4. **Decide how you'll loop in a trusted adult** — before you send it, share the draft with a parent/guardian or your instructor for a quick look.
5. Do **not** send the message during class. Take it home, get it reviewed, and send it with a trusted adult's awareness.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Networking** | Building and maintaining genuine professional relationships with people you know or are warmly introduced to — not mass-messaging strangers. |
| **Warm introduction** | Being connected to a new person through someone you already know and trust, rather than reaching out cold. |
| **Professional etiquette** | The expected norms of polite, clear, respectful communication in a professional context — tone, timing, gratitude, and follow-up. |
