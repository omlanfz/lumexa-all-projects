# Lesson 4: Share What You Know

**Course 18: Professional Presence & Opportunity Building**

## What This Lesson Is About

Today you're writing the first of five short pieces of content you'll create this course — your **Content Piece #1**. This isn't a resume, and it isn't a big formal essay. It's a short, honest post or note where you teach one small, specific thing you actually learned — a tip, a lesson from a project, or a mini-tutorial.

Depending on your track, this will eventually become:
- A **LinkedIn post** (if you're 16+ and using LinkedIn), or
- A **Notes/Blog entry** on your portfolio site from Course 16, or
- A **shared Google Doc** you can send to a teacher, mentor, or program you're applying to.

Today, you're just focused on writing it — formatting for your specific platform comes later.

## Why This Matters

Here's the one rule to remember for all five content pieces:

> **Teach something small and specific — don't try to sound like an expert on everything.**

Nobody trusts "I'm skilled in Python and problem-solving." Almost everybody trusts "I kept getting an error when someone typed a city name with a space in it, and here's the one-line fix that solved it." Specific, honest content is more convincing, more useful to readers, and — good news — much easier to write, because you already have the details. You just have to report them.

## The Content Formula

Every content piece you write, all five, can follow this same three-part formula:

1. **What I was doing** — the task, project, or situation. One or two sentences.
2. **What I learned or noticed** — the specific tip, insight, or mistake-turned-lesson. Two or three sentences. This is the heart of the piece — be concrete.
3. **Why it might help someone else** — one sentence connecting your experience outward to a reader who might face the same thing.

That's it. Three parts. If all three are there, you have a complete content piece.

## Worked Example: Jordan R.

Here's a fictional example from a student named Jordan, who just finished a small project building a to-do list app.

> **Testing your app's "empty state" actually matters**
>
> This week I built a simple to-do list app where you can add and check off tasks. It worked great — until I deleted every task and the app crashed with a blank error screen.
>
> I learned that I had only tested my app with tasks already in the list, so I never noticed that my code assumed there would always be at least one item. The fix was one `if` statement checking whether the list was empty before trying to display it.
>
> If you're building anything with a list — tasks, messages, search results — try emptying it out completely before you call it done. The "nothing here" case is easy to forget and easy to break.

Notice what this example does:
- **Hook**: the bolded opening line pulls the reader in with a specific, useful claim, not a generic one.
- **What I was doing**: one sentence, plain language, no jargon.
- **What I learned**: the specific detail (an empty list crashing the app, the one-line fix) — not "I learned a lot about coding."
- **Why it helps others**: a direct, actionable sentence any reader could apply.
- **Takeaway**: the closing line lands the point in one sentence anyone could remember.
- **Honesty**: Jordan doesn't claim to be an "expert in error handling." Jordan describes one true thing that happened.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Content piece** | One complete short post, article, or note — the unit you're building 5 of this course. |
| **Hook** | The opening line or title that makes a reader want to keep reading — usually the most specific, concrete part of the piece stated up front. |
| **Takeaway** | The closing line that sums up the one useful point you want the reader to remember or act on. |

## Step-by-Step Activity: Draft Content Piece #1

1. **Pick your material.** Choose one real project, class exercise, or coding attempt from the last few weeks — big or small. It's fine if it's small.
2. **Fill in the three formula parts** using the Worksheet (What I was doing / What I learned / Why it might help someone else).
3. **Write a hook.** One sentence or short title that states your specific lesson up front.
4. **Write a takeaway.** One closing sentence that sums up the point.
5. **Assemble the draft.** Put hook → what I was doing → what I learned → why it helps → takeaway into one short piece, about 100–250 words.
6. **Run the self-check** (see Worksheet): does it sound honest and specific, or does it sound exaggerated or vague anywhere? Revise anything that doesn't pass.
7. **Save your draft** in your content-planning doc/sheet, ready for platform formatting in a later step.

## Knowledge Checks

4. Why is a small, specific mistake-and-fix often *more* convincing content than a broad claim of skill?
5. Name the two "bookend" parts of a content piece that go at the very beginning and the very end.
