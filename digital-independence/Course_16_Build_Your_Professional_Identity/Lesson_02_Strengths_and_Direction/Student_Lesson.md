# Lesson 2: Define Your Strengths and Direction

## What You're Doing Today

Last lesson, you found every trace of your work that's already out there — GitHub repos, Lumexa missions, Canva designs, deployed sites. Today you turn that list into something useful: an honest answer to *"what am I actually good at, and what should my portfolio focus on?"*

You are not inventing a personality or picking a job for life. You're reading your own evidence and choosing one honest, provable direction to build your portfolio around for the rest of this course.

## Why This Matters

A portfolio that claims things you can't back up falls apart the moment someone asks a follow-up question. A portfolio built on real evidence — even small evidence — holds up, because you can always show the receipts. This lesson is about finding the receipts you already have.

---

## Three Kinds of "Good At"

It's easy to mix these up. They're not the same thing, and a strong portfolio treats them differently.

| Level | What it means | What counts as evidence | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Interest** | Something you're curious about or enjoy | None required | "I think game design looks cool." |
| **Strength** | Something you're noticeably better at, or improve faster at, than your other work | A pattern across 2+ attempts, or solid feedback | "My CSS layouts always come out cleaner than my other project parts." |
| **Proven skill** | Something you can point to a finished, working thing to demonstrate | One or more completed artifacts | "Here's a Vercel site I built and styled myself." |

**The rule:** your portfolio direction should be led by proven skills, backed up by strengths, and can mention interests only as "what I want to learn next" — never claimed as if they're already true.

## Worked Example (Fictional Student)

> **Note:** "Jordan R." is a made-up example used for teaching. Not a real Lumexa student.

Jordan looked at their project list from Lesson 1:

1. Lumexa Mission "Space Weather Dashboard" — HTML/CSS, styled with Canva graphics, deployed to Vercel
2. Lumexa Mission "Simple Platformer Game" — finished, but the jump mechanic is buggy; Jordan's only game project
3. Personal project — redesigned a birthday invitation in Canva for family
4. Lumexa Mission "Personal Portfolio Starter Page" — HTML/CSS, deployed to Vercel, got positive instructor feedback on layout
5. Group project — helped a teammate fix color scheme and spacing in their CSS (not Jordan's own repo)

**Jordan's honest read:**

- Projects 1, 3, 4, and 5 all involve visual layout and styling — that's the *same skill showing up four times*, across different tools and contexts. That's a real strength.
- Project 2 is a genuine finished game, but it's the *only* one, and it has a known bug. That's an interest with a little early proof — not yet a strength.
- Direction: **front-end web development / visual design** — because the evidence repeats and includes outside positive feedback.
- Game dev goes in the "still want to learn" list — real, but not yet backed by enough evidence to lead the portfolio.

Notice what Jordan did **not** do: claim to be a "full-stack game developer" because one buggy platformer sounded impressive. The direction follows the evidence, not the excitement.

## Step-by-Step Activity

Open your Worksheet and your Lesson 1 audit notes, then work through these steps in order.

### Step 1: List every project

Every Lumexa mission, every personal project, every time you helped someone else with code or design — even unfinished ones. Put each in the inventory table.

### Step 2: Tag what each one demonstrates

For each project, write down the actual skill it shows — be specific ("CSS layout and spacing," not just "coding").

### Step 3: Set an honest confidence level

For each project, mark your confidence: **High** (I could do this again reliably), **Medium** (I did it once, with help or some struggle), or **Low** (I tried, it's rough, or someone else did most of it).

### Step 4: Cluster into a direction

Look for a skill that shows up in **two or more** projects at Medium or High confidence. That repeat pattern is your strength cluster. Pick one (or at most two, if they're closely related) to be your portfolio direction.

### Step 5: Write your direction statement

Use the template on your Worksheet. Keep it to 1–2 sentences, and make sure you can point to specific projects that back it up.

### Step 6: Park your other interests

List things you're genuinely excited about but can't yet prove. These go in "what I still want to learn" — not deleted, just set aside for now.

## Key Terms Glossary

- **Interest** — something you're curious about or enjoy; requires no evidence to claim.
- **Strength** — a skill you're noticeably better at than your other work, shown by a repeating pattern across multiple projects.
- **Proven skill** — a skill backed by at least one finished, working artifact you can show someone.
- **Direction** — the honest focus area you choose to build your portfolio around right now, based on your strongest evidence.
- **Confidence level** — your own honest rating (High/Medium/Low) of how solid your evidence is for a given project.
- **Strength cluster** — a group of two or more projects that all demonstrate the same underlying skill.
