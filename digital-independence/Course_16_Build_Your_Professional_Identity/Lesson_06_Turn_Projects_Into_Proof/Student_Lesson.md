# Lesson 6: Turn Projects Into Proof

## What You're Doing Today

You already have a portfolio skeleton, a bio, one case study, and a brand kit. Today you write two more case studies — bringing you to the 2–3 required — and you decide, using a scoring system instead of a gut feeling, exactly which projects make the final cut.

## Why This Matters

Right now you probably have a list of things you've built. A list of things you've built is not a portfolio. The difference between "a thing I made" and "proof" is what you say about it. Anyone can list "Built a website." That sentence proves nothing — not that you can code, not that you solved a real problem, not that you finished what you started. A reviewer (a mentor, a college, an employer, a client) reading your portfolio is asking one question underneath everything else: *can I trust that this person can actually do the thing?* Vague claims don't answer that. Specific, honest, skill-revealing case studies do.

## What Makes a Project "Proof" — Three Tests

1. **Specificity.** Name the actual tool, the actual constraint, the actual number. "I built a weather app" is a thing you made. "I built a weather app that pulls live data from a public API and caches results so it still works offline" is proof — it names a real technical decision.
2. **Honesty.** Never claim more than what happened. If you worked on a team, say your specific part. If it's not deployed, don't say it's "in production." If a tutorial got you 80% of the way there, say what the remaining 20% was — that's often the most impressive part.
3. **Demonstrated skill.** Every strong case study makes an invisible decision visible. Not just what you built, but why you built it that way, or how you got unstuck when it broke.

## Worked Example: Weak Case Study → Strong Rewrite

**Weak version (a fictional student, "Sam"):**
> **Project:** School Website
> **What I did:** Made a website for my school's robotics club using HTML and CSS. It has information about the club and looks nice. I learned a lot about web design and it was a fun project.

Notice: no specific problem, no specific tool decisions, no specific outcome, no specific skill named. This could describe almost any student's first website.

**Strong rewrite of the same real project:**
> **Project Name:** Robotics Club Recruitment Site
> **Problem/Goal:** Our robotics club had no way to show new students what we do before they showed up to a meeting — sign-ups were low. I needed a site that explained the club fast and made joining easy.
> **Tools Used:** HTML, CSS, GitHub Pages for free hosting.
> **My Approach:** I interviewed three club officers to find out what questions new members actually ask, then built the site around those questions instead of a generic "About Us" page. I used CSS Grid to make the meeting schedule readable on phones, since most students said they'd check it on their phone between classes.
> **Result:** The site went live two weeks before recruitment season. Club sign-ups that mentioned "saw it on the website" went from effectively zero the year before to 9 new members.
> **What I Learned:** That good design starts with asking real users what they need, not guessing. Also learned to debug a layout that broke only on mobile Safari — turned out to be a flexbox default I didn't know about.

Same project. The difference is entirely in what's said about it: a real problem, a real decision (interviewing officers, choosing CSS Grid for a reason), an honest and specific result, and a specific thing learned.

## Step-by-Step Activity

**Step 1 — List every candidate project.**
Write down everything you've built: Lumexa class projects, personal projects, school assignments, group projects. Don't filter yet.

**Step 2 — Score them on the Worksheet.**
For each project, rate Complexity (1–5), Completeness (1–5), and write one sentence for "what it proves." Be honest — an unfinished ambitious idea is not the same as a finished simple one.

**Step 3 — Choose your 2–3 strongest.**
You already have one case study from Lesson 4. Pick your next 2 highest-scoring, most provable projects — not your 2 most complicated ones if they're incomplete, and not your 2 easiest ones if they prove nothing.

**Step 4 — Write two new case studies.**
Use the same template from Lesson 4: Project Name, Problem/Goal, Tools Used, My Approach, Result, What I Learned. Apply all three proof tests as you write.

**Step 5 — Cross-check with your brand kit.**
Pull up your Lesson 5 brand kit. Does the tone/voice of these new case studies match your Lesson 4 one and your bio? Same level of formality? Same style of sentence? A portfolio where every page sounds like a different person is confusing — fix any mismatches now.

## Key Terms

- **Case study:** A structured, specific write-up of one project — problem, approach, result, lesson learned.
- **Proof:** Evidence that lets someone else verify a skill or outcome, as opposed to a claim they have to take on faith.
- **Complexity:** How many real technical decisions a project required.
- **Completeness:** Whether a project actually works end-to-end and can be shown, not just described.
- **Brand consistency:** Using the same tone, vocabulary, and style across every piece of your portfolio so it reads as one person, not several drafts.
