# Lesson 4: Pricing, Scope, and Agreements

## What You're Learning Today

You already have a service package from Lesson 3 — something you could offer to a client. Today you'll learn how to put a fair price on it, how to write down exactly what's included (that's called "scope"), and how to draft a simple agreement so everyone's on the same page.

You'll also learn something important that a lot of people get wrong: **writing something down and signing it does not automatically make it a legal contract.** More on that below — it matters.

## Why This Matters

Freelancers, contractors, and small business owners price their own work every single day. Nobody hands them a price list — they figure it out using simple, honest methods. Learning to do this now, at a small scale with simulated clients, means you'll already know how when it counts for real.

## Pricing, Explained Simply

There are two beginner-friendly ways to price a service:

### Flat Rate
One total price for the whole job, agreed before you start.
- **Best for:** small jobs where you know exactly what's involved.
- **Example:** "$35 for one logo design with two rounds of revisions."

### Hourly Rate
A price for each hour you work, added up at the end.
- **Best for:** jobs where the amount of work is uncertain.
- **Example:** "$12 per hour for tutoring help, however many hours it takes."

**A common beginner method:** estimate how many hours a job will realistically take, multiply by a reasonable reference hourly rate, and use that number to set (or check) your flat-rate price. You'll practice this in the activity below.

## Scope and Price Are Linked

**Scope** = exactly what is included in the job (and what isn't).

If your scope is vague ("I'll design your logo"), a client might expect unlimited revisions and extra requests for the same price. That's called **scope creep** — when the job quietly grows bigger than what was agreed, without the price or timeline changing.

A clear scope protects both you and the client. It's not about being difficult — it's about being clear from the start, so nobody feels surprised or taken advantage of later.

**Vague scope:** "I'll design your logo."
**Clear scope:** "One logo design, two revision rounds, delivered as a PNG and JPG file, within 5 days."

## Worked Example: Pricing Priya's Package

Priya offers Instagram graphic design for small school clubs. Here's how she prices one project:

1. **Scope:** 3 social media graphics, 1 revision round each, delivered as PNG files, within 5 days.
2. **Estimated time:** about 1 hour per graphic (including the revision) = **3 hours total**.
3. **Reference hourly rate:** $12/hour (a reasonable beginner starting point — not a fixed rule).
4. **Math:** 3 hours × $12/hour = $36.
5. **Quoted price:** Priya rounds this to a clean flat rate of **$35** for the whole package.

Notice: Priya used hourly math to *reason her way* to a fair flat-rate quote. This is completely normal — and your price can always shift based on your experience, your local market, and what feels fair.

## The Most Important Part of This Lesson: Agreements vs. Legal Contracts

Read this section slowly. It's the part we spend the most time on in class, for good reason.

When you and a client (even a simulated one) write down what you agreed on — the scope, the price, the deadline — that's a **written agreement**. It's genuinely useful because it:

- Makes sure you both understand the same thing.
- Creates a paper trail you can look back at if there's ever confusion.
- Builds a real skill that adults use in freelance work and business every day.

**But here's the critical part: a written agreement between two people — especially a generic template — is NOT automatically a legally binding contract.** Just because something is written down, and even signed, does not by itself make it enforceable by law.

What actually makes something a real, legally binding contract involves things like:

- **Capacity to contract** — in most places, there are legal rules (often tied to age of majority) about who is legally able to enter into a binding contract. This can specifically affect people under 18.
- **Consideration** — a legal term for something of value being exchanged in a legally recognized way.
- Other legal requirements that are different depending on the country, state, or province you're in.

That's a whole area of law on its own — way beyond this lesson, and not something you need to master to do great beginner client work. What you *do* need is a clear, honest agreement that keeps everyone on the same page. That's what we're practicing.

**One more essential point: if you ever do real client work for real money, a parent or guardian should know about it and be involved** — helping with things like handling payment, communicating with a client if something goes wrong, and making sure the whole arrangement is safe and appropriate for your age. This isn't optional caution — it's simply how paid work should go for someone your age.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Flat rate** | One total price for an entire job, agreed before starting. |
| **Hourly rate** | A price charged per hour of work. |
| **Scope** | Exactly what is (and isn't) included in a job. |
| **Scope creep** | When a job grows beyond what was agreed, without the price or timeline changing. |
| **Agreement** | A written record of what two people understood and agreed to — useful for clarity, not automatically legal. |
| **Legal contract** | A legally enforceable agreement that requires specific legal conditions (like capacity and consideration) which vary by jurisdiction. |
| **Capacity (to contract)** | The legal ability to enter into a binding contract — often tied to age of majority and other rules. |
