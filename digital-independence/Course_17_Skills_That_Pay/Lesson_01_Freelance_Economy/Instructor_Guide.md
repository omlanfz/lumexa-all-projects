## Overview

This lesson is the front door to Course 17. Before students touch any platform, they need a mental model: **a skill becomes a service when someone else is willing to pay for the result, not just the skill itself.** The lesson builds that model through a concrete value-exchange chain (skill → service → payment), contrasts freelance/service work with employment, and gives an honest, factual tour of where this kind of work actually happens today — including the real age and account rules of well-known platforms.

This is explicitly **not** a sign-up lesson. No student creates an account, lists a gig, or is told to bypass a platform's age policy. The goal is literacy and judgment, not premature action. Several platforms require a parent/guardian-owned or co-managed account for minors; this lesson teaches that as a fact of the landscape, not an obstacle to route around.

## Instructor Prep (before class)

- Read the Student Lesson and Worksheet so you know exactly what students will see.
- Confirm the platform rules table in the Worksheet is still accurate by checking each platform's current terms of service or help center page (rules change; do not rely on memory or on this guide alone). Update the "verified as of" date when you check.
- Have blank Google Docs, a Notion page, and a spreadsheet template ready to share/demo — students should see the actual tools, not just hear about them.
- Prepare to project (or share) a simple spreadsheet with columns: Task, Time Estimate, Rate, Total — this is used in the live demo.
- Decide in advance how you will handle a student who says they already have a marketplace account or has already tried freelancing — validate the experience without turning it into a recruitment moment for other students.

## Teaching Sequence (60 minutes)

### 1. Hook and framing (5 min)
Ask: "Has anyone here ever been paid for something they made or did — outside of an allowance or a regular job?" Collect 2–3 quick examples (tutoring a neighbor, selling art online, building a website for a family friend, editing videos for a local team). Point out: these are all small examples of the same economic idea we're studying today.

### 2. What is the freelance economy? (10 min)
Direct instruction using talking points below. Define freelance economy as a system where people offer specific services to clients on a per-project or per-task basis, rather than being hired long-term by one employer.

### 3. The value-exchange chain: skill → service → payment (10 min)
Walk through the three-step chain on the board or shared doc:
- **Skill**: something a person can do (e.g., graphic design, writing, coding, video editing, tutoring).
- **Service**: that skill packaged as something a specific client can buy — a defined, deliverable outcome (e.g., "a 5-slide pitch deck," not just "I know Canva").
- **Payment**: money exchanged for the completed service, agreed on before the work starts.
Emphasize: the jump from skill to service is where most beginners get stuck — they know they can do something but haven't defined what they're selling.

### 4. Employment vs. freelance/service work (8 min)
Use the comparison talking points below. Keep this factual and balanced — neither model is framed as superior.

### 5. Live demo: pricing and delivering a small job (12 min)
Walk through the fictional freelancer case study (script below) using a shared spreadsheet and Google Doc live. Narrate every decision out loud.

### 6. Landscape overview: platforms and models (10 min)
Introduce the five models students will explore on the Worksheet: Fiverr, Upwork, Etsy, Gumroad, and direct referrals. State each platform's general age/account rule factually, and explicitly flag that these rules can change and must be checked at the source before anyone relies on them. Do not demonstrate account creation. Do not encourage sign-up.

### 7. In-class activity + wrap-up (5 min)
Students begin the value-exchange labeling activity from the Student Lesson (finish for homework if needed). Preview that Lesson 2 will go deeper into defining a service and setting scope.

## Talking Points

**On what "freelance economy" means:**
"The freelance economy isn't a single company or website — it's a pattern of work happening across the whole economy, where people sell specific skills as services instead of trading their time to one employer for a steady paycheck. A photographer shooting one wedding, a coder building one small app feature, a tutor helping one student prepare for one exam — all freelance."

**On skill vs. service:**
"Having a skill means you *can* do something. Having a service means you've decided *what exactly* you're offering, *to whom*, and *for what result*. 'I'm good at drawing' is a skill. 'I'll design a custom logo, three concepts, one round of revisions, delivered as a PNG and vector file' is a service."

**On employment vs. freelance (comparison points to cover):**
- Employment: one employer, ongoing relationship, regular schedule/paycheck, employer usually provides tools/training, taxes often withheld automatically, legal protections like minimum wage often apply directly.
- Freelance/service work: multiple possible clients, work is project-based and can start and stop, payment is per-project or per-task and agreed in advance, the freelancer usually supplies their own tools, and — importantly for minors — almost all real marketplace platforms require an adult (parent/guardian) to own or co-manage the account, and some require the freelancer to be an adult entirely.
- Say plainly: "Freelance work is not 'easier' or 'a way around getting a job.' It has its own rules, its own risks, and for anyone under 18, it almost always involves a trusted adult in the account and the money."

**On honesty about the landscape:**
"We're going to look at real platforms people use for this kind of work. Some of them work with the involvement of a parent or guardian if you're a teenager; one is 18-and-up only. We are not signing up for anything today, and I am not telling you to get around any age rule anywhere, ever. Age rules on these platforms exist for real legal and safety reasons, and they change over time — always check the platform directly before assuming a rule still applies."

## Live Demo Script: Pricing and Delivering a Small Job

Use this narrated walkthrough with a shared spreadsheet and Google Doc. Introduce the fictional freelancer by name so students can reference her later.

**Character:** Mara, a fictional 17-year-old who designs simple logo concepts.

**The request (read aloud, as if it were an email):**
> "Hi Mara, I run a small dog-walking business in my neighborhood and need a simple logo — just a paw print and my business name, 'Riverside Walks,' in a clean font. Can you help? — Denise"

**Step 1 — Clarify scope (in the Google Doc, write it live):**
"Before Mara can price anything, she needs to know what 'help' means. She replies to Denise with three questions: How many design concepts do you want to see? Do you need any revisions? What file formats do you need (just an image? or a file a printer can use)?" Type Denise's fictional answers: "One or two concepts is fine, one round of changes, and I'll need a PNG for my website and a vector file for a t-shirt company."

**Step 2 — Define the service (write in the Doc):**
"Two logo concepts, one revision round, final files delivered as PNG and vector (SVG)."

**Step 3 — Estimate time and set a rate (build live in the spreadsheet):**

| Task | Time estimate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Two initial concepts | 2 hours | |
| Client feedback + 1 revision | 1 hour | |
| Final file prep (PNG + SVG export) | 0.5 hours | |
| **Total** | **3.5 hours** | |

"Mara decides her time is worth $12/hour for this kind of work — a number she picked by looking at what friends who do similar design work charge, not a random guess. 3.5 hours × $12 = $42. She rounds to an even **$40 flat fee** so Denise knows the total cost upfront instead of worrying about an hourly clock."

**Step 4 — Confirm terms before starting (write the confirmation email):**
> "Hi Denise, here's what I'll deliver: two logo concepts, one round of revisions, and final files as a PNG and an SVG. The total is $40, due when the final files are delivered. Does that work for you?"

Point out: *the price and scope were agreed on before any work started* — this is the single most important habit in freelance work.

**Step 5 — Deliver and get paid:**
"Mara does the work, sends the two concepts, Denise picks one and asks for a small color change, Mara revises it, and delivers the final PNG and SVG files by email along with a note asking Denise to send payment however they'd agreed (in Mara's case, her parent's account received it, since Mara is a minor and the payment method was set up with her parent)."

Close the demo by asking: "Where in this story did skill become a service? Where did service become payment?"

## Discussion Questions

1. In Mara's story, what would have gone wrong if she'd skipped Step 1 (clarifying scope) and just started designing?
2. Why do you think Mara set a flat fee instead of just charging by the hour with no cap?
3. What's one skill you have that could become a "service" if you defined it clearly? What would the defined service look like (not just the skill)?
4. Why might a platform require a parent or guardian to be involved in a minor's account, even if the teenager is the one doing the actual work?
5. What's the difference between "I want to make money" and "I have a specific service I could offer"?

## Common Misconceptions

**"Freelancing is a get-rich-quick path."**
Correct this directly: freelance income is earned the same way any income is — by delivering something of value, consistently, over time. Mara's $40 job took real hours and real back-and-forth with a client. There is no shortcut version of this that skips the work.

**"I need a marketplace account to start."**
Correct this directly: the *freelance economy* is the pattern of skill-for-payment work itself — it existed before any of these platforms and continues outside them (direct referrals, word of mouth, school or community projects). A marketplace account is one *channel* into that economy, not a requirement for understanding or eventually participating in it. Many teens' first real service work happens through people they already know, with a parent aware of the arrangement — no platform account required at all.

## Anticipated Student Difficulties & Troubleshooting

| Difficulty | What it looks like | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Confusing "skill" with "service" | Student says their service is "I'm good at Photoshop" | Ask: "If I were your client, what exact thing would you hand me at the end?" Push toward a deliverable. |
| Wanting to sign up for a platform right now | Student asks "Can I make a Fiverr account today?" | Affirm the enthusiasm, redirect: "That's exactly what later lessons in this course help you prepare for. Today we're building the thinking, not the account — and any account for someone your age needs a parent or guardian involved anyway." |
| Assuming freelance work is unregulated or risk-free | Student treats age rules as trivia rather than real safeguards | Explain briefly that age rules exist for legal (contract law, payment processing) and safety reasons, and that they differ by platform and country — which is exactly why the Worksheet has a "verify" column. |
| Anchoring on unrealistic prices | Student guesses a price with no reasoning ("$500 for a logo") | Walk them back to the time-estimate method from the demo: hours × a reasonable rate, not a guessed number. |
| Discomfort naming their own skill | Student says "I don't have any skills people would pay for" | Prompt with categories: writing, design, tutoring, tech help, organizing, video/photo editing, translation. Most students have at least one. |

## How to Review Student Work

- **In-class activity (value-exchange labeling):** Check that each student correctly identifies which part of their example is the skill, the service, and the payment — and that the "service" is a specific deliverable, not a restated skill.
- **Worksheet (Freelance Economy Landscape):** Check that the age/account rules column reflects what was taught in class (not a guess), and that the "verified as of" field is filled in with an actual date rather than left blank. The reflection questions should show the student reasoning about their *own* current situation (age, access to a parent/guardian's involvement, etc.) rather than giving a generic answer.
- Look for signs a student thinks they should sign up for a platform immediately — if so, follow up individually and reinforce the misconception correction above.

## Lesson Close

Summarize in one sentence: "Today we learned that a skill becomes valuable when it's turned into a clearly defined service someone else will pay for — and that there are many real ways this happens, some requiring a parent or guardian's involvement if you're under 18, and some not requiring a platform at all." Preview Lesson 2: defining a service and setting a scope of work in detail. Remind students the Worksheet is due before the next class.
