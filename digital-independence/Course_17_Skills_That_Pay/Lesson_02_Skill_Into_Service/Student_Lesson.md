# Lesson 2: Turn a Skill Into a Service

**Course 17: Skills That Pay — Digital Independence Path**

## What This Lesson Is About

In Course 16, you built a portfolio. In Lesson 1 of this course, you learned how the freelance world works — the platforms, the client types, the basics of pricing.

Now it's time to answer a harder question: **what, exactly, would you offer someone?**

"I know coding" or "I'm good at design" is true, but it's not something a client can say yes to. Today you'll turn one skill you already have proof of into a **service** — a specific, describable thing someone could actually hire you for.

## Why This Matters

Nobody hires "a person who knows HTML." They hire someone who can say: "I'll build you a 4-page website for your tutoring business by next Friday." That sentence is clear enough to trust. Vague skills don't get hired — clear services do.

This lesson is the bridge between "I can do things" and "here is a thing you can pay me to do." Everything after this — pricing, finding clients, proposals — depends on getting this part right first.

## Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Service** | A specific, packaged version of a skill that someone can hire you to do — it has a clear output, a clear audience, and clear limits. |
| **Niche** | A focused slice of a broader skill area — instead of "design," a niche might be "social media graphics for student clubs." |
| **Scope** | The boundaries of what's included in your service (and what isn't) — how much, how long, how many revisions. |
| **Target client** | The specific type of person or group your service is built for — not "everyone," but a named group you understand. |

## The Skill → Service Formula

```
SERVICE = SKILL + SPECIFIC OUTPUT + SPECIFIC AUDIENCE + SCOPE BOUNDARY
```

- **Skill** — something from your Course 16 portfolio you have real evidence of.
- **Specific output** — the actual thing the client receives (a website, a document, a set of graphics, a cleaned spreadsheet).
- **Specific audience** — who actually needs this and might realistically hire a teen for it (student clubs, tutors, small local groups, other students, family-adjacent contacts).
- **Scope boundary** — a limit: how many pages/items, how long it takes, or what's *not* included.

## Worked Example: Jordan R.

Jordan's Course 16 portfolio includes three proven pieces:
1. A personal website built with HTML/CSS
2. A set of presentation slide designs made in Canva for a school debate team
3. A spreadsheet Jordan built to track and chart a class fundraiser's donations

Applying the formula to each:

| Skill | Service Idea |
|---|---|
| HTML/CSS | "I build simple 3-page websites for school clubs, delivered in one week, with one round of revisions." |
| Canva slide design | "I design a matching 10-slide presentation deck for a student team's competition or pitch, turned around in 3 days." |
| Spreadsheet tracking | "I set up a spreadsheet that tracks and charts a club's fundraiser or event signups automatically, so organizers don't have to update it by hand." |

Notice: each one names an output, an audience, and a limit. None of them say "I do design" or "I'm good with spreadsheets."

## Step-by-Step Activity

You'll complete this in the **Skill-to-Service Builder Worksheet** (Notion or Google Docs — whichever your instructor assigned).

**Step 1 — List your skills.**
Open your Course 16 portfolio. Pick 2–3 skills you have real, finished work to back up. Write them in the skills inventory table.

**Step 2 — Draft 2–3 possible services.**
For each skill, use the formula to draft at least one possible service. Aim for 2–3 total service ideas across your skills. Don't worry about picking the "best" one yet — get options down first.

**Step 3 — Pick your strongest one.**
Look at your options. Ask yourself:
- Which one can I describe in one sentence without hesitating?
- Which one is closest to something I could actually start doing soon?
- Which one has the clearest audience — do I know where to even find these people?

Write your final choice into the "My Chosen Service" statement using the template in the worksheet.

## Two Mistakes to Avoid

**"My service has to work for everyone."**
Wrong. A service for "everyone" is actually a service for no one — it's too vague to say yes to. Naming a specific audience (a club, a type of student, a small local group) makes your service *easier* to sell, not smaller.

**"My service has to be completely unique."**
Also wrong. Plenty of people build websites or design graphics. What makes a beginner service work isn't originality — it's being specific, reliable, and easy to understand. You can develop your own style over time; you don't need it to start.
